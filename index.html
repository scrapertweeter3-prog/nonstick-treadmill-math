A $20 nonstick pan is a subscription with a two year term

The nonstick aisle sells the same skillet five times a decade and prints each price like it is the only one. The coating on a $20 pan is planned around weeknight use, and weeknight use is what a kitchen gives it: eggs on Sunday, stir fry on Wednesday, a metal spatula once by accident. Two years in, the surface stops releasing and food starts grabbing, which is the failure mode the low price was quietly counting on. Run the decade straight: five pans at $20 is $100. A $30 cast iron skillet bought once carries the same decade for $30, a third of the treadmill's bill.

Per dinner the spread holds. Four cooked meals a week is 208 uses a year, 2,080 a decade. The nonstick treadmill costs 4.8 cents a use. The iron costs 1.4. The iron's whole decade price is one and a half pans, so the second pan you buy is the one that carries the treadmill past the buy-it-once price, and three more follow before the ten years close. The warranty fine print tells the same story from the other side: a typical nonstick warranty runs one to two years, ending almost exactly where the coating does, while cast iron makers commonly stamp theirs lifetime, because there is almost nothing on the pan that can wear out. A scratch in a coating is a replacement; a scratch in seasoned iron is a re-seasoning.

Nothing here says the $20 pan cooks badly on day one. It says the shelf prices per pan and the kitchen pays per decade, and only one of those numbers is printed on the tag. The knob is buying the $30 object once and letting the fifth pan never happen.
